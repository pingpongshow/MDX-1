MDX-1 firmware

The detector engine, screen interface and hardware layer for the MDX-1. The engine is portable C compiled and exercised on this machine against a physics simulator of the coil, ground and targets; the STM32H743 layer is written but awaits a bench. This guide covers what the firmware does beyond the commercial units, how the signal path works, the test evidence, the screens, and the bring-up plan.

Signal path, one transmit period at a time

Numbers that define the behaviour

  • Detection threshold = noise floor × 3 × 20^((30 − sensitivity)/29): sensitivity 30 sits at 3× the tracked floor, sensitivity 1 at 60×.
  • Noise floor: tracked from |T| when no target is present (2 % per period at start-up, 0.2 % after), only from samples under 3× the floor.
  • Ground tracker time constant: 8 s stationary, 1 s at 1.5 m/s swing; frozen for 0.6 s after any signal over twice the threshold.
  • Recovery 5 (default): 16-period fast average (3 ms), reference 384 periods (75 ms).
  • Target estimate is updated only while the signal is 3 dB above threshold; the last good estimate is held on the weak edges so the displayed ID does not flicker.

Coherence and the period length

One period is M ADC samples and M × 448 timer ticks; the transmit table is rebuilt for each M so its 80 slots sum exactly to that. The DFT of one period at bins 1, 2, 3, 4 and 8 is therefore leak-free at any M, and the ratio to the transmit-current channel removes the coil's inductance change with soil, rail sag and filter delay. The simulator confirms the demodulated transfer impedance to 4 parts in 10⁵.

Test evidence

The simulator generates the two 24-bit ADC streams the hardware would produce: the exact RL response of the transmit winding to the slot table (edges at timer-tick resolution), the coil's own per-line phase and residual null, viscous and salt ground, dipole-coupled targets with a relaxation-frequency model, white noise at 20 µV rms (the electronics alone give about 1.2 µV; the extra stands in for environmental noise), a CW interferer, and a sinusoidal 0.4 m sweep with the IMU reading the swing rate. PI records are synthesised the same way with 1/t viscous ground.

6

Coil wizard

  1. Air, L and R. With the coil in the air the wizard regresses di/dt against i inside the constant-voltage slots of the transmit waveform. That gives the inductance from the intercept and the resistance from the slope, independent of the rail voltage.
  2. Air null. The five slow-averaged line ratios are stored as the residual; from now on they are subtracted.
  3. Ferrite. A ferrite rod held at the coil centre is a purely magnetic target. Its response per line defines the "magnetic" direction; the wizard stores the unit phasor that rotates it onto the real axis, so ground and target models see the same coordinates whatever the coil's phase shifts.
  4. Coin at 10 cm. The signal strength becomes the depth reference (and, in Hybrid mode, the PI depth reference stored in the coil record).
  5. Save. Written to the coil's DS2431 with a CRC, or to the detector's memory for chip-less coils.

Phone link and companion app

The Bluetooth audio module also carries a small serial channel (BLE "Transparent UART"). The firmware speaks a framed protocol on it (core/link, spec in docs/MDX-1_link_protocol.md): a PIN handshake, the detector's own menu as an item table (name, range, labels, action), live status at up to 20 frames per second, actions (noise cancel, ground grab, pinpoint, learn, mark find, coil wizard, save, power off), the find log with positions, learned targets and diagnostics. Because the phone asks for the menu instead of hard-coding it, new firmware settings appear in the app without an app update. The bench exercises the whole protocol through a loopback transport (28 checks) and writes a captured status frame that the app's unit test decodes.

The iOS app (app/ios, SwiftUI + CoreBluetooth, iOS 17) builds with xcodegen and Xcode; it has no other dependencies. The Android app (app/android, Kotlin + Jetpack Compose, minSdk 26) is a screen-for-screen port with the same protocol code and demo detector; it was written on a machine without the Android SDK and has not been compiled yet. Headless use: with no UI board fitted the detector boots into the same state, plays a three-note cue on the headphones, and the app is the only interface; every function on the detector's own menu is reachable from the phone. On the target the BM83 host-command opcodes in stm32/bm83.c are marked VERIFY and must be checked against Microchip's BM83 UART command set at bring-up.

STM32 bring-up plan

Hardware interface change the firmware forced (schematic rev A1)

Two 24-bit converters at 512 kSPS on one SPI bus would need 25 Mbit/s with a chip-select transaction every 1.95 µs. The ADS127L01's frame-sync slave mode instead takes a continuous bit clock and a frame pulse and streams the data like a TDM audio codec, and the STM32H7's SAI peripheral is exactly that master. Rev A1 daisy-chains the two converters (B into A's DAISYIN), drives SCLK and FSYNC from SAI1 block B at 32.768 MHz and 512 kHz, and receives 64-bit frames by circular DMA with no CPU involvement. The schematic, netlist check and ERC were re-run after the change.

Order of work on the bench

  1. Clocks and rails: confirm 458.752 MHz core, 16.384 MHz on MCO2, VTX follows the DAC (8 to 30 V).
  2. TIM1 waveform into a resistive dummy load; scope the five lines; check dead time and the break input.
  3. SAI frame: verify slot edge, first-bit offset and sign extension against one converter fed a DC level, then the daisy chain.
  4. Period alignment: confirm sample 0 of each period lands on slot 0 (the DFT phase of the transmit-current channel must be constant period to period).
  5. Coil wizard on the real coil; compare L and R with an LCR meter.
  6. Air tests, then ground; adjust the noise-floor and threshold constants if the real environment noise differs from the 20 µV assumption.
  7. PI mode last: 100 V rail, bleed, fault trip.
